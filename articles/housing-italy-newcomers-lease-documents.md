# Renting a Home in Italy as a Newcomer: Leases, Deposits and the Documents You Need

> How renting works in Italy for non-EU professionals: temporary first, long lease second, what landlords ask for, and how the lease supports residence and family files.

Housing is the part of the move that worries people most, and for good reason. Italian landlords often prefer tenants with an Italian payslip and an Italian guarantor, and in the big cities good flats go quickly. With a little planning it is manageable. Here is how we suggest newcomers approach it.

## Two steps, not one

Very few people sign their final lease from abroad. The usual pattern is:

1. **Temporary accommodation** for the first weeks: a serviced apartment, a furnished flat on a short contract, or a residence. It gives you an address for the consulate, the stay contract and the first appointments.
2. **A long-term lease** once you know the city, the commute and the neighbourhood. This is the address you register at the municipal registry and, if your family is joining, the one checked for reunification.

Book the first before the visa appointment; consulates ask for proof of accommodation, such as a lease, a hotel booking or a hosting declaration.

## What a landlord will ask

- your passport and permit or permit receipt;
- your tax code;
- your employment contract;
- recent payslips, or a letter from the employer confirming salary and start date;
- sometimes a guarantor or a larger deposit.

The employment contract is your strongest asset. A permanent Italian contract with a known employer reassures landlords far more than an offer from a foreign company. When we are your employer, we issue the contract and an employer letter as soon as the permit is granted, and payslips from the first month.

## Lease types in brief

Italian leases come in a few standard forms: long open-market leases, "agreed rent" leases with terms set by local agreements, and transitional leases for temporary needs. Each has its own minimum duration and notice rules. Read the termination clause carefully, especially if your contract or your plans may change. We do not quote durations here; ask the agent or a local adviser for the current rules of the lease you are offered.

## Deposits and agency fees

Expect a security deposit and, if you use an agency, an agency fee. Both can be significant. Ask your employer whether the relocation package covers them, or whether an advance can be arranged. Housing paid or reimbursed by the employer is common in relocation packages; its tax treatment depends on how it is structured, so agree it in writing.

## Housing and the family file

If your spouse or children will join you, the home must meet health and hygiene standards and be suitable for the number of occupants. The municipality checks it as part of the reunification process. A one-bedroom flat that suits you alone may not pass for a family of four.

Choose the long-term home with the final household in mind, and keep the lease, the floor plan and any certificates the agent can provide.

## Where to look

- Large listing portals and local agencies for the open market.
- Company housing lists or colleagues, often the fastest route.
- University cities have many furnished flats, but competition with students peaks before the academic year.
- Commuter towns: many people working in the big cities live outside them and commute by train, with lower rents and larger homes.

## Red flags

- A landlord who refuses a written lease or asks to be paid in cash.
- A lease that cannot be registered: you need a registered lease for residence registration and for the family file.
- Deposits requested before you have seen the flat or met the owner or agent.

## Small print, plain answers

**Can I sign a lease before I arrive?** It is possible, but risky without seeing the flat. A short temporary stay first is safer.

**Will a landlord accept a foreign employer?** Many prefer an Italian employer. An Italian contract and payslips from us solve that.

**Can my employer be the tenant?** Companies sometimes rent flats for staff. It changes the tax treatment and the residence registration. Discuss it with us before agreeing.

**How long does a search take?** In the big cities, good flats go quickly, and landlords often decide within days. Have your documents ready in a folder before you start viewing, so you can make an offer on the spot.

**Is furnished or unfurnished better?** Furnished flats are easier for the first year, especially in Milan and university cities. Unfurnished flats can be cheaper for longer stays, but you will need to buy kitchen appliances in some cases.
